**Assessment:** **No**

## Full write-up

### 1. Vote: No, based on this record

The documents establish a real loss of school parking and a potential benefit from keeping students on campus. They do **not** establish that a roughly 240-space deck is the best response at the proposed financial commitment.

I used only the local files and checked the scanned figures against the supplied images. The ballot description and city–school funding split come from the task prompt; the official ballot language and signed funding agreement are absent from these files.

### 2. Top three reasons

1. **The counts do not establish a broad parking shortage.** Across 29 observations on three dates, total occupancy peaked at **939 of 1,310 spaces, or 71.68%**, leaving 371 vacant. Public-city parking never exceeded **65.70%** occupancy. These aggregate figures cannot establish availability near a particular entrance, but they weaken the case that hundreds of additional spaces are necessary. Utilization CSV, lines 11–25

2. **The engineer recommended trying other options first, and the subsequent qualification remains unresolved.** The November memo recommended proceeding without parking improvements, considering a bus pilot, then expanding the middle-school lot if problems emerged, and considering a deck after other options. The February follow-up says some options may be infeasible but identifies neither the options nor their specific limitations. That leaves a material gap in the justification for choosing the deck. Traffic memo, PDF pp. 3–4/images 3–4, February follow-up, PDF p. 1/image 1

3. **The commitment is substantial relative to the demonstrated need, with important obligations unspecified.** The city’s projected repayment is **$13,212,287**, including **$4,212,287 interest**. Under the older site plan, a 240-space deck would provide approximately **120 net additional school-system spaces** after accounting for replacements and losses. Operating responsibilities, capital reserves, public access and a confirmed total project budget are missing. Debt schedule, lines 2–21, Traffic memo, PDF p. 2/image 2, Parking map, PDF p. 5/image 5

### 3. What would change my vote

I would switch to Yes if additional primary documents established all three:

- **A recurring, consequential shortage:** location-specific counts covering school arrivals, dismissal, evening activities and major events, with projections after the expansion.
- **A persuasive comparison of alternatives:** a revised engineering analysis explaining which alternatives fail, their costs, and why this deck produces better overall safety and access after accounting for queues and pedestrian conflicts.
- **A complete financial and operating commitment:** a verified project budget and final space count, plus a signed city–school agreement covering overruns, operations, major repairs, reserves and enforceable public access.

A deck could be justified by dependable campus access and reduced street crossings even with aggregate vacancies. Those benefits need to be measured against the alternatives and costs.

## Its independent city memo, written before seeing the others

**Decision.** Pause the city’s construction and borrowing commitment and pursue alternatives first. Keep the certified November 3 question before voters; if approved, proceed only after the conditions below are satisfied.

### Why

- The three surveyed dates peaked at 939 occupied spaces out of 1,310, but school parking reached 86.8% on Saturday. Aggregate spare capacity does not establish convenient availability near the school (inferred); the planned 71-space loss deserves a solution (utilization CSV; November 10 traffic memo).
- The engineer recommended existing street parking first, middle-school expansion next, and a structure afterward. The February follow-up identifies unspecified feasibility constraints without endorsing this deck; require those constraints to be explained (November 10 memo; February 4 letter).
- Full city borrowing models approximately $13.21M in debt service (inferred, debt-schedule CSV). Applying the supplied $611-per-space operating benchmark to 240 spaces yields $146,640 annually, excluding depreciation (inferred, April 23 operating-cost email). No executed agreement or complete project-specific operating budget is supplied (fact-check of this guide).

### Conditions or changes required

Price and compare a smaller deck, shared parking, middle-school expansion, and transportation pilots with safer crossings. Require current location-specific demand and construction-period access plans.

Before borrowing or awarding construction, require an itemized, bid-supported budget—including signals, accessibility, contingency and escalation—and an executed agreement covering ownership, school funding, overruns, public hours, fees, maintenance and repair reserves. Specify funded pedestrian/micromobility improvements rather than relying on aspirational benefits (research summary).

### Next steps

- **Before Nov 3:** City manager and finance director, October 19: publish factual costs, alternatives and evidence gaps. Commission and city attorney, October 26: adopt enforceable spending conditions by resolution, with their legal effect **(to confirm with city attorney)**. Counsel should review limits on public-resource ballot advocacy; publish neutral information (research summary §2).
- **If it passes:** City manager, schools and engineers, January 31, 2027: complete comparisons and agreement. Commission: decide whether and how much to borrow after reviewing bids; permissible redesign scope **(to confirm with city attorney)**.
- **If it fails:** City manager and schools, December 15, 2026: present a funded access and alternatives plan.

### Risks and how to manage them

The memo predicts morning backups and 30–60-minute discharge; require updated queue analysis, delivery access and neighborhood lighting mitigation. Address construction-period needs regardless of the vote (traffic memo; research summary §5, secondary).

### What would change the decision

Evidence that feasible alternatives cannot meet documented demand, coupled with safe operations, timely delivery, secured funding and enforceable public benefits, would justify conditional construction.
