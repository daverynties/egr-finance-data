Quick guide · about 2 minutes

What you’re voting on

On Nov. 3, voters decide if the City of East Grand Rapids can borrow up to $9 million. The money would pay the City’s share of a parking deck at the high school on Bagley Avenue, plus new traffic signals and safety work for people who walk and bike. A property tax would repay the loan over up to 20 years.

What it costs you

The tax is based on your home’s taxable value. You can find it on your tax bill. It is usually half of your home’s market value or less.

The strongest point on each side

Points for YES

  • The school loses 71 parking spaces for good. Only about 65 would be left on campus during construction.
  • Students who park in the deck would not have to cross a street. The deck would also add public parking for events and Gaslight Village.
  • The schools would share the cost, and voters get the final say.

Points for NO

  • The City’s traffic consultant said to try cheaper fixes first, like street parking and the middle-school lot. A later letter said some may not work but did not say which.
  • At the busiest hour in 2025, 371 of 1,310 spaces in the area were empty.
  • Key terms are not signed, such as who pays extra costs and when the public can park there.

What your vote does

A YES vote

The City may borrow up to $9 million for this project. The City Commission still has to approve the bids, a deal with the schools and the loan itself. It can borrow less.

A NO vote

The City cannot borrow this money. The earliest it could ask voters again is May 2027. The schools say the vote will help them decide whether to go ahead or pause.

Questions still open

  • Who pays if the deck costs more than the roughly $16 million named so far?
  • Who runs and repairs the deck, and with what money? About $160,000 a year has been mentioned.
  • Will the public be able to park there during school hours, and will it be free?
  • Can the deck open before the 2028–2030 construction crunch?

Executive summary

The decision in one screen

The proposal links a parking structure at EGR High School with traffic signals and pedestrian infrastructure. The case turns less on today’s utilization than on what happens when school construction removes spaces from 2028–2030.

$15.2M–$18.1M

What is proposed

A three-level, 240-space deck, plus signals and pedestrian work. The City would bond up to $9M. The school board voted 5–1 on April 20, 2026 to set aside about $7M from its general and capital improvement funds.21 The $9M city bond plus the school’s ~$7M set-aside totals about $16M. That sits below four of the six OAK estimates ($15.2M–$18.1M); no document says who covers overruns. The OAK range covers the deck alone, while signal, loading-dock and pedestrian work is not separately itemized.

371 empty

What the counts found

The 2025 study’s system-wide peak was 71.7%: 939 of 1,310 spaces occupied. School lots reached 81% on a weekday and 86.8% on Saturday.8

~190 net

What the deck adds

After replacing the 49-space Bagley lot, the 240-space deck adds about 191 spaces versus the post-construction, no-deck condition. inferred22

Key points

The 2025 counts found room system-wide (71.7% at the busiest hour) but tight spots near the high school. The expansion permanently removes 71 campus spaces, and the district projects about 65 campus spaces during 2028–2030 construction. Not yet settled: a signed city–school agreement, who pays overruns and upkeep, public access during school hours, and any street-parking changes.

01NeedCurrent capacity is available; construction changes the campus math.

The 2025 utilization counts do not show a system-wide shortage on the measured dates. At the busiest observed system hour, 939 of 1,310 spaces were occupied—71.7%—leaving 371 open.8 The school lots reached 81% on the measured weekday and 86.8% on Saturday; the commonly used 85% benchmark was not reached system-wide on the weekday.8 The 85% figure is a commonly cited industry rule of thumb; no primary source in the record establishes it.

Pressure was much sharper closest to school: the 15-space San Lu Rae street segment reached 233.3% at 2 p.m. on May 8, and the 111-space senior lot reached 106.3% at 1 p.m. on March 12.8 The study does not say how many of the 371 open spaces were within walking distance of the high school. On public access, Commissioner Groff-Blaszak describes the design as having one level reserved 24/7 for public use; no signed agreement sets the split.13

The high-school expansion changes the campus supply. The senior lot had 111 spaces; the expansion plan retains 40, creating a 71-space reduction.2 That shortfall comes from the separately approved high-school expansion, rather than from a measured shortage across the full district today.2

Key primary-source finding
Build the high-school expansion without new parking.

That was the Progressive Companies memo’s recommendation. It found on-street capacity within 1,500 feet and said a structure should be considered only after other options were explored. It also warned that a 30–60 minute exit could push students back to on-street parking.5

In a Feb. 4, 2026 follow-up letter, the same engineer wrote that “after additional consultation with the City and schools, the schools review noted that some options and recommendations may not be feasible due to certain on-campus limitations.” The letter does not say which options.5

Annual ownership cost

The City has not published a breakdown of its operating estimate. The closest record is a deputy city manager’s April 23 email citing Grand Rapids’ $611 per space per year, “not depreciation,” or $130,143 for the deck then under discussion (213 spaces). At 240 spaces that is about $147,000. inferred6 The Shared Parking Manual the City circulated puts basic operations at $175 and a repair sinking fund at $60 per space per year in 2018 dollars, about $70,000–$75,000 a year today for 240 spaces. inferred16 Adding 1% of construction cost as a repair reserve gives an upper case near $300,000–$330,000. inferred These benchmark scenarios span about $70,000–$330,000 a year for the whole deck, or $35,000–$165,000 for a half share. inferred They are not a City budget, and no dedicated funding source is identified.

Commissioner Schwartz said July 20: “the school needs two levels and the third is a possibility for the City.”4 The April memo says a two-story deck meets the school’s no-net-loss goal, at about 213 campus spaces.2 That implies a two-story deck of about 118 stalls, so the 240-stall design adds about 122 beyond it. Under the planned 50/50 split, the City’s half of the $15.2M–$18.1M range is about $7.6M–$9.1M, or about $62,000–$74,000 per additional stall. inferred This is an allocation comparison, not a priced cost for the third level; no two-story price has been released. If the school’s contribution stays at its ~$7M set-aside, the remaining cost would be about $8.3M–$11.1M, more than the $9M bond at the high end. inferred

What $160K means

About $160,000 a year has been cited for the whole deck’s annual operations, not the City’s half, and that exact figure does not appear in the cited sources. The closest source-backed calculation is $611 × 240 spaces = $146,640 for the full structure, excluding depreciation. An even split of that would be $73,320; an even split of the cited $160,000 would be $80,000. inferred6

Authorizes the City to issue up to $9 million of these bonds for the stated scope. The Commission still has to approve construction bids, a city–school agreement and the bond sale, and may borrow less.12

If it grows at the City model’s assumed 2% a year, the 20-year total is closer to about $2,360–$3,070. inferred

The City reported $6,511,815 in unassigned general-fund balance, equal to 44.01% in the FY2025 ACFR and above its 20%–25% target.19 That balance is less than a $7.6M–$9.1M City share. Using part of it could reduce borrowing but would lower the balance toward the 20%–25% target, and it would not fund annual operations.

Local tax context

EGR’s 2024 city levy was 14.09 mills (operating 11.35, extra-voted 1.73, debt 1.01)—high among nearby Kent County cities, though below Cedar Springs at 18.77 and Lowell at 15.95.20 This comparison is context, not a measure of service equivalence or total household tax burden.

What residents wrote

The August 3 packet reproduces seven handwritten May 27 comment cards: three opposed or concerned from Bagley Avenue residents, a fourth opposed card that is unsigned and lists no address, one supportive card from Greenwood Avenue, and two offering ideas without taking a position. It also includes three emails: one unsigned form letter against the project, forwarded by the mayor as “an email campaign,” and two asking for a public vote. The city manager summarized the response as “mostly opposition ... especially those who live in close proximity.” Names and street numbers are omitted here.1

Subcommittee record

The joint city–school subcommittee had less than a quorum of either board, so the Open Meetings Act did not apply and there is no record of its meetings’ content. City memos and presentations describe some of its conclusions.13

Late resolution

Commissioner Groff-Blaszak wrote that about 90 minutes before the August 3 meeting, the City Manager emailed commissioners a 33-page document with new ballot language, requested by two commissioners, that added “pedestrian safety” to the proposal.13

Pedestrian scope

Pedestrian-safety language was added in August, but no project list or dedicated budget was released.1

Agreement not signed

No signed city–school agreement has been made public as of this guide’s review date. Until one is signed, cost share, ownership, upkeep and public access are not binding.

Gaslight history

The debate is shaped by the loss of a prior private parking structure in Gaslight Village and earlier development commitments. That history shapes public views, but it does not by itself establish the need, cost or legal obligations of this proposal.

07Risks & open questionsThe ballot sets borrowing authority; several implementation choices remain open.

Cost escalation

The estimate assumes roughly 3.5% annual escalation—more than $500,000 for a year of delay on a $15M-plus project. inferred1

Interest-rate exposure

The modeled true interest cost rose from 3.33% to 4.13% across estimates. Final debt service depends on market conditions when bonds are sold.7

Undefined extras

Signal work, loading-dock accommodation, snow-melt systems and final facade choices are not fully itemized in the released estimate.

Behavior risk

No student survey tests whether drivers will choose the deck, especially if departure queues are long or event pricing is later introduced.

Joint Recommendation of the AI Review Panel on the Bagley Parking Deck Bond Proposal

Adopted October 8–9, 2026 · Regarding the $9 million Public Parking Facility and Pedestrian Safety Bond Proposal, November 3, 2026 ballot

Panel: Grok; Claude (claude-opus-5-5); Codex (gpt-6.1-sol, high reasoning)

Findings

  1. The high-school expansion removes 71 campus spaces, and about 65 spaces remain on campus during 2028–2030 construction.518
  2. At the busiest 2025 hour, 371 of 1,310 area spaces were empty, but school lots reached 86.8% on one Saturday, so spare spaces elsewhere do not prove convenient parking at the school.8
  3. The City’s traffic consultant ranked a structure last, after on-street parking and the middle-school lot. The Feb. 4, 2026 follow-up says some options “may not be feasible” but names none.5
  4. No City–school agreement is signed. Free public use is promised in writing only for school-event attendees, and no operating budget line exists.186
  5. Deck-only estimates run from $15.25 million (plain concrete, 2026 dollars) to $18.11 million (brick, 2028 dollars). The $9 million plus about $7 million covers the low option and falls up to about $2.1 million short of the high one (panel’s arithmetic).121

Recommendation

The Panel recommends that the certified question go to voters on November 3. The City should sell no bonds and award no construction contract until every condition below is met. Borrowing should be sized to the bids, and the City Attorney should confirm the chosen project fits what voters approved. Whatever the vote, the City and schools should deliver a 2028–2030 student-access plan.

Conditions

  1. A signed City–school agreement covering the cost split, a cap on the City’s share, school responsibility for its own overruns, the split of running and reserve costs, enforceable public hours with no-fee public use, and the 3-foot right-of-way.
  2. A published itemized estimate and real bids, with borrowing limited to what the bids require.
  3. A priced side-by-side comparison: a two-level deck, the plain-concrete Option 5, the middle-school lot, on-street management plus busing, and a no-deck alternative. Any brick upgrade is paid by the schools.
  4. A funded 20-year running and reserve budget. The record supports about $56,000–$147,000 a year; the long-term repair reserve still needs an engineer’s estimate.
  5. The schools’ contribution confirmed, plus a priced list of the walking and biking projects.
  6. An exit and queue design for the 30–60 minute after-school load-out, and a construction schedule measured against 2028–2030.

If the measure fails

By December 15, 2026, the City Manager and the schools deliver a funded access plan that follows the engineer’s sequence: on-street parking, then the middle-school lot. Any new ballot measure waits until the conditions are met, and no earlier than May 2027. (If it passes, the comparison, a draft agreement and a student survey are due by January 31, 2027.)

Recorded differences

  • What to call it. Codex preferred calling it a “pause, alternatives first.” Claude and Grok preferred “proceed with conditions.” The text sets the substance and uses neither label.
  • Default size. Claude preferred a two-level, plain-concrete deck by default unless the schools pay for the third level. Codex wanted two and three levels priced separately first. The text leaves the size to the comparison.
