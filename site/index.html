November 3 ballot: the money questions.

East Grand Rapids voters face two tax questions on November 3, 2026, alongside the midterm races for governor, U.S. Senate, U.S. House and the state legislature.

The whole ballot in 30 seconds

Two proposals would affect local property-tax bills.

One would finance a city parking and pedestrian-safety project. The other would renew and increase Kent County’s jail millage.

City proposal Up to $9 million · up to 20 years

About $148 a year on a home with $300,000 in taxable value at the average millage — about $2,960 over 20 years. The city’s bond analysis estimates about $13.2 million in total debt service.

County proposal 0.98 mills · 2026–2045

A renewal plus a 0.2468-mill increase. About $294 a year on a $300,000 home, including about $74 from the increase. Estimated first-year revenue: $37.3 million. An organized vote-no campaign exists.

City parking + pedestrian safety bond

  • What it would build: a 240-space public parking deck on Bagley at the high school site; traffic signals at Lake and at Bagley/San Lu Rae; and pedestrian and micromobility safety infrastructure.
  • Ballot name: “Public Parking Facility and Pedestrian Safety Bond Proposal.” It is not approved; voters decide November 3.
  • Millage: an estimated 0.5922 mills in the first year and a 0.4925-mill simple average over the life of the bonds — about $49 a year per $100,000 of taxable value.
  • Why a $9 million bond for a $15–18 million deck: the project is a 50/50 partnership with East Grand Rapids Public Schools, which report $7 million available from prior millages. The bond covers the city’s share; the city sizes the bond after construction bids.
  • Not included in the bond: about $160,000 a year in ongoing deck maintenance and operations. That would come from the city budget.

Kent County jail millage

0.98 mills for 2026–2045 to expand, remodel, equip, operate and maintain Kent County correction and detention facilities, and to pay debt service on them.

It renews the existing millage, which would otherwise expire after the 2029 levy, and increases it by 0.2468 mills. Estimated first-year revenue is $37.3 million.

An organized vote-no campaign exists at griid.org.

What residents are asking

Does the city’s own parking study say parking is adequate?

At a June 2026 work session, the city’s consultants, Progressive Engineering, reported that no part of the system exceeded the 85% occupancy benchmark used to signal tight supply. That is accurate, with an important qualification: the finding uses system-wide averages across on-street and off-street spaces, not peak event nights in Gaslight Village.

Why should I pay $3,000 for something I’ll never use?

The 20-year total on a home with $300,000 in taxable value is about $2,960 at the average millage. The math behind the claim checks out; whether the project is worth that cost is the voter’s call.

What happened to the earlier underground garage plan?
Needs additional documentation

No public record was found explaining what happened to the prior concept for an underground garage beneath the science building.

When was pedestrian safety added to the ballot language?

Pedestrian-safety and micromobility language was introduced with revised ballot language adopted by the commission in August 2026 on a 4–1–2 vote. Some residents have called it a late addition; supporters say it reflects the full project scope.

Could the Lake/Bagley signal be built without the bond?

The bond includes a roughly $500,000 placeholder for the signal. Whether the city could or would fund it separately is a commission decision and is not settled in the records.

Bottom line for residents

The city takes in slightly more than it spends, holds reserves well above benchmarks, and runs a self-sustaining water utility.

What counts as spending

Net expenditures across governmental funds, Water & Sewer and Motor Equipment, excluding transfers between city funds and GASB conversion entries. Health care, pension and retiree-health trusts are reported separately to avoid double counting.

Fiscal-year dates

East Grand Rapids runs July 1–June 30. FY2025 means July 1, 2024 through June 30, 2025. FY2026 year-to-date figures stop March 31, 2026 — 75% through the year.

What a flag means

A neutral prompt for context: above budget at an interim date, a large year-over-year move, a vendor spike, concentration, a large payment or detail absent from the supplied files. It is never an allegation.

What the data cannot show

The check register omits payroll, ACH, wires and P-card purchase detail. No FY2024-dated check register was provided. Interim budget comparisons cannot establish a final overrun.

About this page.

The full provenance: what this project is, where every number came from, how it was checked and what the available records cannot establish.

Independent, plain-English financial reporting

This page explains where City of East Grand Rapids money comes from, where it goes, what changed and what questions are worth asking. It is not affiliated with the city government, a campaign or an advocacy group. It starts without a predetermined conclusion; when the finances look well managed, it says so.

Where the numbers come from

  • Nine financial spreadsheets obtained by FOIA on June 15, 2026 from City Clerk Lori Parmenter: the chart of accounts; three years of fund-transfer reports; two check registers; FY2024 and FY2025 general-ledger transaction extracts; and year-to-date budget detail through March 31, 2026. The city redacted check numbers before release.
  • City of East Grand Rapids FY2024 Annual Comprehensive Financial Report for the year ended June 30, 2024, published at eastgrmi.gov and used for debt, pension and retiree-health figures.
  • The adopted FY2025–26 city budget, published at eastgrmi.gov.
  • Michigan Department of Treasury local-finance data and audited financial reports for East Grand Rapids, Grand Rapids, Kentwood, Wyoming, Walker and Grandville. The comparison uses FY2024 audited actuals for all six.
  • Millage records: the city’s official June 2026 millage spreadsheet and Kent County records.
  • Parking bond records: official city commission minutes and adopted ballot language. The proposal is on the November 3, 2026 ballot and is not approved.

How the numbers were checked

The headline FY2025 spending figure of $26,702,474 was computed from the general ledger and cross-checked against the audited financial report’s governmental-funds total; they match within $6. Check-register totals were verified against report footers to the cent. Every figure on this page traces to a source listed above.

“Needs additional documentation”

This label appears wherever the available records could not verify the reason behind a figure. It is not an accusation. It means the paperwork needed to confirm the explanation was not among the files obtained.

What this page cannot tell you

  • Individual employee pay; the files contain twice-monthly payroll summaries, not individual paychecks.
  • Vendor names for some capital projects.
  • FY2024-dated check activity; the check files received begin in July 2024.
  • The city’s internal reasons for budget variances; the records provide the numbers, not management explanations.
