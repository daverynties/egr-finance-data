November 3 ballot: the money questions.

East Grand Rapids voters face three tax questions on November 3, 2026, alongside the midterm races for governor, U.S. Senate, U.S. House and the state legislature.

The whole ballot in 30 seconds

Three proposals would affect local property-tax bills.

One would finance a city parking and pedestrian-safety project. Two county questions cover the jail and a renewal for the zoo and museum. The ballot also includes two statewide proposals: a constitutional convention question and a campaign-finance proposal.

City proposal Up to $9 million · up to 20 years

About $148 a year on a home with $300,000 in taxable value at the average millage — about $2,960 over 20 years. The city’s bond analysis estimates about $13.2 million in total debt service.

County proposal 0.98 mills · 2026–2045

A renewal plus a 0.2468-mill increase. About $294 a year on a $300,000 home, including about $74 from the increase. Estimated first-year revenue: $37.3 million.

County renewal 0.4086 mills · 2026–2035

Renews funding for John Ball Zoo and Grand Rapids Public Museum. About $123 a year on a home with $300,000 in taxable value. Estimated first-year revenue: $15.6 million.

City parking + pedestrian safety bond

  • What it would build: a 240-space public parking deck on Bagley at the high school site; traffic signals at Lake and at Bagley/San Lu Rae; and pedestrian and micromobility safety infrastructure.
  • Ballot name: “Public Parking Facility and Pedestrian Safety Bond Proposal.” It is not approved; voters decide November 3.
  • Millage: an estimated 0.5922 mills in the first year and a 0.4925-mill simple average over the life of the bonds — about $49 a year per $100,000 of taxable value.
  • Why a $9 million bond for a $15–18 million deck: the project is a 50/50 partnership with East Grand Rapids Public Schools, which report $7 million available from prior millages. The bond covers the city’s share; the city sizes the bond after construction bids.
  • Not included in the bond: about $160,000 a year in ongoing deck maintenance and operations. That would come from the city budget.

Kent County jail millage

0.98 mills for 2026–2045 to expand, remodel, equip, operate and maintain Kent County correction and detention facilities, and to pay debt service on them.

It renews the existing millage, which would otherwise expire after the 2029 levy, and increases it by 0.2468 mills. Estimated first-year revenue is $37.3 million.

Kent County zoo + museum millage

0.4086 mills for 2026–2035 for John Ball Zoo and Grand Rapids Public Museum.

This is a renewal, not a new tax. It would cost about $123 a year on a home with $300,000 in taxable value and is estimated to raise about $15.6 million in its first year.

What residents are asking

Does the city’s own parking study say parking is adequate?

The June 2026 consultant study reported no part of the system exceeded the 85% occupancy benchmark. That finding uses system-wide averages across on-street and off-street spaces. It does not reflect peak event nights in Gaslight Village.

What happens if the bond fails?

The parking deck is not built. The city and schools would need another plan for event parking, and the existing lots would remain.

Why a parking deck instead of more surface parking?

The site is constrained. A deck adds about 240 spaces on the existing footprint; surface expansion would require land the city does not have at that location.

Why should I pay $3,000 for something I’ll never use?

The 20-year total on a home with $300,000 in taxable value is about $2,960 at the average millage. The math behind the claim checks out; whether the project is worth that cost is the voter’s call.

What happened to the earlier underground garage plan?
Needs additional documentation

No public record was found explaining what happened to the prior concept for an underground garage beneath the science building.

When was pedestrian safety added to the ballot language?

Pedestrian-safety and micromobility language was introduced with revised ballot language adopted by the commission in August 2026 on a 4–1–2 vote. Some residents have called it a late addition; supporters say it reflects the full project scope.

Could the Lake/Bagley signal be built without the bond?

The bond includes a roughly $500,000 placeholder for the signal. Whether the city could or would fund it separately is a commission decision and is not settled in the records.

What counts as spending

Net expenditures across governmental funds, Water & Sewer and Motor Equipment, excluding transfers between city funds and GASB conversion entries. Health care, pension and retiree-health trusts are reported separately to avoid double counting.

Fiscal-year dates

East Grand Rapids runs July 1–June 30. FY2025 means July 1, 2024 through June 30, 2025. FY2026 year-to-date figures stop March 31, 2026 — 75% through the year. The provided check-register filenames are off by one fiscal year, so figures use actual check dates.

What a flag means

A neutral prompt for context: above budget at an interim date, a large year-over-year move, a vendor spike, concentration, a large payment or detail absent from the supplied files. It is never an allegation.

What the data cannot show

The check register omits payroll, ACH, wires and P-card purchase detail. No FY2024-dated check register was provided. Interim budget comparisons cannot establish a final overrun.

Why schools are separate

A Michigan school district is its own government — not a city department. East Grand Rapids Public Schools has its own elected school board, levies its own property taxes, adopts its own budget, and gets its own independent audit. The city commission has no say over school spending, and the school board has no say over city spending. The funding model is different too: nearly two-thirds of school revenue comes from the state per-pupil formula, while the city runs mostly on local property taxes. That's why this site treats them separately — different books, different government.

Source for the funding mix: Michigan Department of Education state FID, FY2024–25 actuals.

How students are doing

Test scores come from the Education Recovery Scorecard (Stanford/Harvard), which converts state M-STEP results to a national scale. Score = grade levels above/below the 2019 national average, averaged over math and reading, grades 3–8.

  • EGR students score about 2 grade levels above the national average — top 5% of U.S. districts in both math and reading.
  • Lowest chronic absenteeism in the peer group: 8.3%, vs. 32.1% statewide.
  • One soft spot: the learning-rate trend ranks in the 4th percentile nationally. Scores are high, but growth has slowed more than at peer districts.
  • Income gap: low-income students score +0.44 grade levels vs. +2.17 for non-low-income. Both groups rank in the 90th+ percentile nationally within their group.

Similar districts: Birmingham (+2.15), Forest Hills (+2.14), Rockford (+1.82). Michigan statewide: −0.71.

Source for every figure in this section: Education Recovery Scorecard 2025.
